Finance & Unit Economics for AI

The number that decides your fate is gross margin, and your cost of goods is tokens and GPUs.

20 min read · Part V - Company Building

Software companies got fat on 80% gross margins because the marginal cost of the next customer was a rounding error. AI companies do not get that gift. Every request your product serves burns real compute, and that compute lands in cost of goods sold, not in the R&D line where investors forgive it. This chapter is about the one number that quietly decides whether your beautiful AI product is a business or a science project: the margin left after you pay for the tokens.

13.1AI COGS: inference, not just servers#

In a classic SaaS profit-and-loss statement, cost of goods sold (COGS) is hosting, a support team, and some third-party tooling — a thin, mostly fixed layer under a torrent of near-free revenue. In an AI company, COGS grows almost linearly with usage, because the dominant term is inference: the compute you spend generating each response. The industry has re-learned this the hard way. The average AI product shipped a 52% gross margin in 2026 versus the ~80% that defined mature SaaS, and inference alone consumes roughly 23% of revenue at scaling-stage AI B2B companies.1 Traditional SaaS runs total COGS of 10–25% of revenue; AI puts a single line item at the top of that range before you have paid for anything else.2

Your AI COGS has five buckets, and founders who only budget the first one get blindsided by the other four:

COGS bucket What it is Typical share of AI COGS Scales with
Inference Token generation (API or self-hosted GPU) 50–70% Requests × tokens/request
Retrieval & data Vector DB, embeddings, re-ranking, search calls 8–15% Corpus size × query volume
Orchestration & tooling Agent framework hosting, tool/API calls, function runners 5–12% Steps per task
Observability & evals Tracing, logging, LLM-as-judge scoring in prod 3–8% Request volume
Human-in-the-loop Escalation agents, labeling, review of low-confidence output 5–20% 1 − automation rate
11The nastiest hidden cost is evals in production (see Chapter 10). If you run an LLM-as-judge on every response to catch regressions, you have just added a second inference call to every request — double your token bill unless you sample.

The mental shift is this: inference is a variable input cost, like flour for a bakery, not a fixed asset like the oven. You cannot amortize it away with scale. If anything, it grows with success. Two structural choices determine its size, and both live in other chapters: which provider and model you buy (see Chapter 7) and how you architect routing, caching, and context (see Chapter 9). Here we only turn those choices into numbers.

Definition

AI COGS — the sum of all usage-variable costs incurred to serve one unit of product value: inference tokens, retrieval, tool calls, in-production evaluation, and any human review. If a cost rises when a customer uses you more, it belongs in COGS, not R&D.

13.2Unit economics: cost and margin per action#

Seat-based pricing is a lie for AI products, because a “seat” that fires 5,000 agent runs a month costs you fifty times more to serve than one that fires 100. The only honest unit of analysis is the action: one resolved ticket, one generated document, one completed agent task. Compute your fully-loaded cost per action, compare it to the price you charge for that action, and you have your true unit economics.

Cortex, our autonomous support agent for mid-market SaaS companies, sells on exactly this unit: price per resolved ticket. This is the emerging standard. Intercom’s Fin charges $0.99 per resolved conversation;6 Zendesk launched outcome pricing at $1.50 per automated resolution on committed volume ($2.00 pay-as-you-go); Sierra charges roughly $1.50 per resolved interaction and rode that model from $100M to $200M ARR and a $15.8B valuation; Decagon pairs a ~$50K platform fee with a custom per-resolution rate.45 Cortex prices at $1.20 per resolved ticket — cheap enough to undercut a human ticket (which costs the customer $15–$35 fully loaded) by an order of magnitude, expensive enough to have a business, if the cost side cooperates.

Now the cost side. A single resolved ticket is not one model call — it is a conversation. Cortex’s naive v1 architecture, running everything through a frontier model, looked like this:

text
Cortex cost per resolved ticket — naive (frontier-only)
  intent classification        1 call   ~2K tok   $0.03
  retrieval + rerank           2 calls  ~1K tok   $0.02
  reasoning / tool selection   3 calls  ~18K tok  $0.34
  answer drafting + revision   2 calls  ~9K tok   $0.21
  in-prod eval (judge)         1 call   ~4K tok   $0.08
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  inference subtotal                              $0.68
  retrieval infra + tool APIs                     $0.09
  observability                                   $0.02
  human escalation (18% of tickets × $0.22 amort) $0.04
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  fully-loaded COGS per resolved ticket           $0.83

At $1.20 price and $0.83 cost, Cortex’s contribution margin is $0.37, or 31%. That is a company that dies at scale: every dollar of new revenue drags 69 cents of cost, sales and G&A eat the rest, and the burn multiple never converges. The founders’ instinct — “we’ll fix it with volume” — is exactly wrong, because there is no volume discount on a variable input.

22In the wildIntercom Fin's honesty clause - In 2026 Fin still charges $0.99 only when it fully resolves a conversation; if the AI hands off to a human, the customer pays nothing for that interaction. It is elegant marketing, but it is also a brutal unit-economics constraint: Fin eats the full inference cost of every attempt, including the failures it does not bill for. Your cost-per-action denominator is total actions; your revenue numerator is only the successful ones. Price the gap in.

Founder's Rule

Price per outcome, cost per attempt. Your margin lives in the gap between the two — measure it before you sign the contract, not after.

13.3The gross-margin reality of AI products#

Here is the uncomfortable table every AI founder should stare at. Gross margin is not a vanity metric; at exit it is a valuation multiplier, because a 70%-margin dollar of revenue is worth far more than a 50%-margin dollar.

Product archetype Typical gross margin (2026) Why
Thin API wrapper, frontier model, no routing 25–45% Every request pays full frontier price; no moat
Routed multi-model app + caching 55–70% Cheap models absorb easy traffic
Fine-tuned / distilled model on owned infra 65–80% Lower per-token cost, amortized training
Workflow SaaS with light AI feature 70–82% AI is a small fraction of total value delivered
Traditional SaaS (reference) 78–85% Compute is a rounding error

Investors have caught on. In 2026 they check gross margin “more carefully than before to make sure AI inference costs are not quietly eating your profit margin”; a GPT-wrapper at 50–60% invites questions about defensibility, while proprietary or fine-tuned systems that clear 70%+ get the premium.13 For a SaaS company bolting AI onto an existing product, the typical hit is 12–17 points of gross margin — enough to reset a company’s valuation category.3

The way out is not to charge more (the market caps your price) but to engineer your COGS down. The single highest-leverage move is model routing: send the easy majority of requests to a small, cheap model and reserve the frontier model for the hard minority. Route the easy ~60% down and you save roughly 38% of inference spend with no measurable accuracy penalty; aggressive setups with semantic caching report 50–70%.910 (The how — cascades, confidence thresholds, routers — is Chapter 9’s territory. Here we only bank the savings.)

Cortex did exactly this. It added a router: a fast, cheap model (DeepSeek-V4 class, ~$0.14/$0.28 per 1M tokens8) handles intent, retrieval, and the 62% of tickets that are routine password-reset-and-billing questions end to end. Only genuinely ambiguous tickets escalate to a frontier model (Claude Opus 4.8 class, $5/$25 per 1M7). Semantic caching kills repeat questions outright. The rebuilt cost stack:

text
Cortex cost per resolved ticket — routed + cached
  cheap-model path (62% of tickets)      blended  $0.11
  frontier path (38% of tickets)         blended  $0.29
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  blended inference per resolved ticket           $0.18
  retrieval infra + tool APIs                     $0.06   (cache cuts calls)
  observability (sampled 20%, not 100%)           $0.01
  human escalation (11% × $0.22 amort)            $0.02
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  fully-loaded COGS per resolved ticket           $0.27

Same $1.20 price, new $0.27 cost: contribution margin jumps to $0.93, or 78%. Cortex went from a doomed 31% product to a fundable 78% product without touching its price or its accuracy target — purely by treating inference as an engineering cost to be optimized, not a bill to be paid. That is the whole game.

13.4Burn, runway and compute budgeting#

AI startups burn differently from SaaS, and the difference is compute. GPU spend consumes 40–60% of technical budgets in the first two years, and AI startups burn roughly 2× what SaaS companies spend on infrastructure.2 Your runway math has a term SaaS never had: a compute line that can spike with a viral week or a single runaway agent loop.

Compute budgeting splits into two very different problems. Inference compute scales with revenue and should be modeled as a variable COGS (dollars per action × actions). Training / fine-tuning compute is lumpy capex-like spend you incur ahead of revenue. If you self-host, an H100 runs a market-median $2.29–$3.12/hr in 2026 — as low as $1.38/hr on verified neo-clouds, but $6.88/hr on AWS p5 and ~$12/hr on Azure.1112 A single H100 running 24/7 is $1,500–$3,000/month; an eight-GPU fine-tuning cluster is $12,000–$24,000/month.12 Provider choice alone is a CFO decision, not just an infra one (see Chapter 7).

33In the wildThe neo-cloud arbitrage - In 2026 the cheapest verified on-demand H100 sat at $1.38/hr while AWS charged over $7.50/hr for the identical chip — roughly a 5× spread. A startup spending $10K/month on H100 compute pays about $4,680/month less on a specialized provider than on a hyperscaler, exceeding $56,000 in preserved runway over a year. For a pre-revenue AI company, that is a month of extra life bought with a config change.

Three disciplines keep compute from eating your runway:

  • Underwrite COGS at build time. Before you ship a feature, estimate its cost per action at expected volume. A feature that is margin-negative at scale should be re-architected or repriced before launch, not after the invoice arrives.
  • Set token budgets and hard caps per request and per customer. An agent with no step limit is an unbounded liability; one pathological ticket looping through a frontier model can cost more than a hundred normal ones. Cap steps, cap tokens, alert on anomalies.
  • Exploit credits, but do not build on them. GPU credit programs and provider startup deals routinely offer $100K–$350K in compute; take them, but model your unit economics at list price, because credits expire and your margins must survive the cliff.
44Prepaid API commits and reserved GPU capacity trade flexibility for a 20–40% discount. Only commit once your usage is predictable — an early over-commit is just burn with extra steps.

Warning

Compute credits are a runway extender, not a business model. Founders who show 75% gross margins that secretly depend on $250K of expiring cloud credits are not lying to investors — they are lying to themselves. Model everything at list price and treat credits as a bonus.

13.5Financial modeling and the metrics investors probe#

Build your model bottom-up from the unit, never top-down from a TAM percentage. Start with cost and price per action, layer in volume growth, model the COGS curve bending down as routing and caching mature (a defensible, dated assumption you must justify), then stack fixed costs to get to burn and runway. The single most important output is the trajectory of gross margin over time — investors want to see a credible path from today’s 50s to the 70s, driven by specific engineering levers, not hope.

The metrics an AI-literate investor will probe in 2026, and where you need to land:

Metric 2026 benchmark What it reveals
Gross margin 60–80% (AI-native); 50–60% flags wrapper risk Whether inference is under control13
Burn multiple (net burn ÷ net new ARR) Series A median ~1.2×; AI-native 0.8–1.2× Capital efficiency13
Revenue per employee $300K+ premium; $150K min at Series A AI leverage on headcount14
Rule of 40 (growth % + margin %) 40 minimum; best VCs want 50+ Growth-efficiency balance15
Cost per action & trend Falling quarter-over-quarter Margin durability
Gross-margin-adjusted CAC payback < 18 months Whether thin margins break the CAC math

Two AI-specific traps hide in these numbers. First, CAC payback must be computed on gross-margin dollars, not revenue — at 50% margin, a customer takes twice as long to pay back as the top-line suggests, so thin AI margins quietly wreck otherwise-healthy sales efficiency. Second, watch NDR versus COGS growth: usage-based AI revenue expands beautifully, but if a customer’s usage doubles and your cost to serve them doubles with it, net-dollar retention flatters a P&L that is not actually getting more profitable. The good news: AI-native companies are already posting burn multiples of 0.8–1.2×, beating traditional SaaS at nearly every stage, precisely because automation holds headcount down while revenue compounds.13

For Cortex, the model that raises a Series A is legible: 78% and rising gross margin (with a stated path to low-80s as the router improves), cost per resolved ticket falling ~15% per quarter, revenue per employee north of $400K because the product is the labor, and a burn multiple under 1.0×. Every one of those numbers traces back to the same discipline — engineering inference down instead of paying it as a bill. That is what makes it an AI company worth funding rather than a SaaS company with a token problem. The fundraising story built on top of these numbers is Chapter 14.

Founder's Rule

Model your unit economics at list price, on gross-margin dollars, with a COGS curve you can defend line by line. Everything an AI investor believes about your company, they infer from that one spreadsheet.

Key Takeaways

Key takeaways - In AI companies, inference is a variable COGS that scales with usage — it cannot be amortized away, and it is why 52% gross margins, not 80%, are the 2026 norm. - The honest unit of analysis is the action (a resolved ticket, a generated doc), not the seat. Compute fully-loaded cost per action against price per action. - You price per outcome but pay per attempt — margin lives in the gap, widened by failed and escalated actions. - Model routing plus caching is the highest-leverage margin lever: it moved Cortex from 31% to 78% margin at an unchanged price, without hurting accuracy. - Compute credits are runway, not a business model — model everything at list price so your margins survive the cliff. - Investors in 2026 probe gross margin, burn multiple, revenue per employee, and cost-per-action trend; compute CAC payback on gross-margin dollars.

Exercise

Hands-on: Build your cost-per-action waterfall 1. Pick your single most important product action (the thing you charge for). Instrument one week of production traffic to capture, per action: number of model calls, tokens in/out per call, and which model served each. 2. Multiply by current provider prices to get inference cost per action. Add retrieval, tool-call, observability, and human-escalation costs to reach fully-loaded COGS per action. 3. Divide COGS by your price per action → contribution margin. If it is below 60%, you have a routing/architecture problem, not a pricing problem. 4. Classify your traffic: what fraction of actions are “easy” and could be served by a model 10–30× cheaper? Estimate the blended inference cost if you routed that fraction down. 5. Rebuild the waterfall with routing and caching applied. The delta between the two waterfalls is your margin-improvement roadmap — put dates on each lever. 6. Project cost per action forward four quarters assuming falling model prices (~30–50%/yr) and improving routing. Hand that curve to your model as the COGS assumption behind gross margin.
